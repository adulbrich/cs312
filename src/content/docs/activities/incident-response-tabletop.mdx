This activity puts into practice the concepts from the [Incident Response and Postmortems](/lectures/incident-response-and-postmortems/) lecture. Instead of analyzing a fictional scenario, you will walk through the GitLab.com database outage of January 31, 2017, using the public postmortem as your source. By the end, you will have a single sheet of paper that records every framework decision the lecture introduced: severity, phases, mitigations, recovery criteria, communications, and a blameless postmortem skeleton with owned action items.

## What You Will Need

- A single blank sheet of paper (front and back) and a pen or pencil.
- The [Incident Response and Postmortems](/lectures/incident-response-and-postmortems/) lecture, open on a second screen or printed for quick reference. You will look at it often.
- Roughly 80 minutes of working time. Each section below is time-boxed; the instructor will keep the class on pace.

Before you begin, divide your sheet of paper into six numbered sections, one per step below. Leave the bottom-right quarter of the back of the page empty for the final postmortem.

## The Case Study

On January 31, 2017, GitLab.com experienced a major outage that lasted over eighteen hours and resulted in permanent data loss. The team published a remarkably honest public postmortem afterward, which is part of why this incident is still used to teach incident response a decade later.

### The Situation

GitLab.com runs on a PostgreSQL database with a primary-replica setup. The primary handles all write traffic. The replica is kept in sync via streaming replication and exists so that the team can fail over to it if the primary is lost. On the afternoon of January 31, the team had been investigating recurring load issues they initially suspected were driven by spam activity. GitLab later concluded that a background job deleting a mis-flagged employee account also contributed to the spike.

### The Timeline

The following events are reconstructed from GitLab's public postmortem. Some timestamps are approximate.

| Time (UTC) | Event |
|---|---|
| 17:20 | An engineer manually takes an LVM snapshot of the primary so a fresh copy can be loaded into staging. It is not intended as a disaster-recovery backup, but it will become the newest usable recovery artifact. |
| ~19:00 | GitLab.com begins experiencing elevated database load initially suspected to be spam. Comments and merge request actions slow noticeably for users. |
| ~23:00 | An engineer notices that the PostgreSQL secondary is severely lagging. The WAL segments needed to catch up have already been recycled on the primary, so replication cannot recover on its own. |
| ~23:00 to ~23:30 | Engineers attempt to rebuild the secondary. They wipe its data directory and run `pg_basebackup`, which appears to hang. They raise `max_wal_senders` from 3 to 32. PostgreSQL refuses to restart because `max_connections` is set to 8000 (too high for the host's resources); they reduce it to 2000 and try again. |
| ~23:30 | An engineer intends to clear the data directory on the secondary in preparation for another rebuild attempt. They accidentally run the command on the **primary** instead. The process is terminated within a few seconds, but by then about 300 GB of data has already been removed. |
| ~23:30 onward | Engineers search for a backup. They discover that the regular `pg_dump` job has been silently failing because it is using PostgreSQL 9.2 against a PostgreSQL 9.6 database, and that the failure emails had been rejected by the receiving mail server for DMARC reasons. Azure disk snapshots were not enabled on the database hosts. The only recent usable recovery artifact is the 17:20 LVM snapshot that had been copied into staging. |
| +18 hours | The team restores from the 17:20 LVM snapshot. Recovery is slow because the usable copy lives on lower-performance staging storage. |
| February 1, ~18:00 | GitLab.com service is restored. |

### What Was Lost

Data created between 17:20 and about 23:30 UTC on January 31 was permanently lost: approximately 5,000 projects, 5,000 comments, and 700 new user accounts. Git repositories and wikis were stored separately and survived intact.

## Step 1: Establish Severity (about 5 minutes)

Severity is not a property of the incident as a whole; it can change as new information arrives. Your first job is to assign severity at two different moments and notice how it shifts.

<Steps>

1. On your paper, label section 1 **Severity**. Underneath, write two rows: **At ~19:00** and **At ~23:30**.

2. Re-read the lecture's severity scheme (SEV-1 through SEV-4). For the **~19:00** row, write the severity you would assign at the moment GitLab.com first shows user-visible database slowness, and a one-sentence justification grounded in user impact and SLO burn.

3. For the **~23:30** row, write the severity you would assign in the minute after the primary deletion is discovered, again with a one-sentence justification. Pay attention to the data-integrity dimension; it is different from latency.

4. Underneath both rows, write a single sentence answering: **when in the timeline did severity need to change, and what signal would have triggered the change in a well-run response?**

</Steps>

<ActivityQuestion>
The lecture argues that severity thresholds should map to measurable signals rather than gut feelings. For the ~19:00 moment, what specific measurable signal (in error rate, latency, burn rate, or business impact) would you set as the threshold that promotes this incident from SEV-3 to SEV-2 or SEV-1? Why would gut-feel reasoning ("this feels bad") have under-reacted here?
</ActivityQuestion>

## Step 2: Map the Timeline to Phases (about 10 minutes)

The lecture's five phases (detect, triage, mitigate, resolve, learn) are postures, not stopwatch segments. Your second job is to mark when the response shifts from one posture to another, and to notice where it should have shifted but did not.

<Steps>

1. Label section 2 of your paper **Phases**. Draw a table with two columns: **Time** and **Phase**. Copy the timestamps from the case study into the Time column.

2. For each timestamp, write the phase the response was in (or should have been in) given the lecture's definitions. The first row is filled in below as a worked example:

   | Time (UTC) | Phase |
   |---|---|
   | 17:20 | (pre-incident: manual snapshot for staging; not yet a response) |
   | ~19:00 (load spike noticed) | Detect |
   | ~23:00 (replication lag confirmed) | ? |
   | ~23:00 to ~23:30 (rebuild attempts) | ? |
   | ~23:30 (primary deletion) | ? |
   | ~23:30 (search for backups) | ? |
   | +18 hours (LVM restore in progress) | ? |
   | Feb 1, ~18:00 (service restored) | ? |

3. Underneath your table, mark with arrows the **two points where the team's posture should have shifted but did not**. For each, write a one-sentence note explaining what the team kept doing when they should have stopped doing it.

</Steps>

<Aside type="note" title="Phases overlap, but not infinitely">
You will notice that some rows could plausibly belong to more than one phase, especially the long rebuild attempt. That is normal: the boundaries are about which posture the team is **primarily** in, not about a clean handoff. Pick the dominant posture and move on; the point of this exercise is to notice the shifts, not to argue about the line.
</Aside>

<ActivityQuestion>
The lecture says the hardest part of the lifecycle is noticing when a posture needs to shift. Looking at your phase table, between which two rows is the most consequential missed shift in this incident? What evidence at the time would have justified the shift, and what would a different posture have done with that evidence?
</ActivityQuestion>

## Step 3: Choose a Mitigation (about 15 minutes)

You are now the on-call engineer at 19:00, when GitLab.com first shows user-visible database slowness. You hold the Incident Commander role until further notice. The actions of the Operations Lead and Communications Lead are your own internal monologue: you must decide what they would each be doing.

<Steps>

1. Label section 3 of your paper **Mitigation Plan**. The lecture introduced five mitigation tools: **rollback**, **feature flags**, **traffic shifting**, **scaling**, and **graceful degradation**. Write all five down the left edge of your sheet.

2. For each of the five tools, write a one-line answer to the question: **does this tool apply here, and if so, what specifically would it look like in this incident?** Some of the answers will be "no, because..." and that answer is valuable; it tells you what part of the mitigation toolkit was simply unavailable to the team.

3. Of the tools that do apply, circle the one you would try **first** at 19:00. To the right of your circle, write its **reversibility**, **blast radius**, and **time-to-undo** in three short phrases. These three principles are the lecture's check for whether a mitigation is sound.

4. Before you would have executed your chosen mitigation, you need to define what success looks like. Write your **recovery criteria** in one sentence: a specific, measurable statement of when you would consider this incident mitigated. The lecture's Aside on this is worth re-reading. A recovery criterion that says "things look better" is not a recovery criterion.

5. Finally, write one sentence comparing your plan to what actually happened. Did the GitLab team define recovery criteria before they committed to the secondary rebuild path? What evidence in the timeline tells you?

</Steps>

<Aside type="tip" title="Mitigation versus understanding">
If your instinct in step 3 was to write "investigate why replication is lagging before doing anything," notice that the lecture explicitly warns against this posture during mitigation. Diagnosis is slow and users do not benefit from your understanding. Pick the fastest reversible action that has a real chance of reducing user impact, even if you have not fully diagnosed the cause yet.
</Aside>

<ActivityQuestion>
The actual mitigation the GitLab team eventually used was an LVM snapshot restore. Rate this mitigation on the lecture's three principles: reversibility, blast radius, and time-to-undo. Why is it on the opposite end of the spectrum from a Kubernetes rollback, and what does that tell you about the kind of mitigation a team should design **for** rather than fall back to?
</ActivityQuestion>

## Step 4: Draft the Communications (about 15 minutes)

By 19:30, the response has been running for thirty minutes and the Communications Lead role needs output. You are still the IC, so the communications below are drafts you would hand to a Comms Lead. The discipline is to produce them anyway, in writing, before you need them.

<Steps>

1. Label section 4 of your paper **Comms**. Below the heading, draw two boxes: **External (status page)** and **Internal (incident channel)**.

2. In the **External** box, write a 2 to 4 sentence status page update suitable for posting **30 minutes** into the response, when you know there is elevated database load and user-visible slowness but do not yet know the cause. Apply the lecture's four rules: describe what customers observe, do not speculate about cause, say what you are doing about it, set the time of the next update.

3. Read your draft aloud to yourself once. Cross out any phrase that violates one of the four rules. Notice especially any internal vocabulary (service names, hostnames, "WAL segments", "pgpool") that leaked into the customer-facing version.

4. In the **Internal** box, write a 3 to 5 sentence pinned channel summary suitable for the same moment, this time for engineers. Include the current severity, the IC's name (yours), the current customer impact, the leading hypothesis, the mitigation in progress, and the time of the next update.

5. Underneath both boxes, write one sentence answering: **what fact appears in your internal version that you intentionally kept out of the external version, and why?**

</Steps>

<Aside type="caution" title="Never speculate publicly">
The lecture's caution is worth restating before you draft: a public status update should never say "a junior engineer ran the wrong command" or "we believe the issue is in pgpool" or anything else that broadcasts blame or speculation outside the response. The technical detail belongs in the postmortem after the dust settles, written with care. The public update during the incident describes only symptom, impact, and what you are doing.
</Aside>

<ActivityQuestion>
Imagine that two hours into the response, you still do not know the root cause, and customers on social media are starting to demand details. The pressure to publish a hypothesis is real. What language does the lecture give you for resisting that pressure while still showing that the team is engaged? Write the one-sentence holding update you would post at that two-hour mark.
</ActivityQuestion>

## Step 5: Time-Box Under Uncertainty (about 10 minutes)

The lecture's chapter on decision-making under pressure introduces time-boxing as a structural check against the System 1 failure mode of staying anchored on a hypothesis that is not yielding. In this incident, the team had already been operating under load pressure for hours by the time the secondary rebuild began around 23:00, and the `pg_basebackup` path still continued long enough to set up the deletion mistake at about 23:30. A different IC posture could have forced an earlier re-evaluation.

<Steps>

1. Label section 5 of your paper **Time-Box**. Re-read the lecture's section on **Time-Boxing** in the Decision-Making Under Pressure chapter; one paragraph is enough to ground this step.

2. Imagine yourself as IC at **23:10**, ten minutes into the rebuild attempt that is not progressing. Write down the **explicit time-box** you would have set for that investigation: a specific clock time after which you would force a re-evaluation regardless of progress.

3. Below your time-box, write the **next action** that would have been taken when the box expired. The lecture suggests the IC should pre-commit to that next action before the box starts, so the responders are not deciding under sunk-cost pressure at the moment it expires.

4. Now identify **one earlier moment** in the timeline where a time-box would have prevented escalation. Mark the time on your paper and write a sentence describing what would have changed.

5. Finally, write one sentence answering: **what cognitive trap is time-boxing protecting against, and why is the IC the right person to enforce it rather than the Operations Lead?** The lecture's two-systems framing is the vocabulary to use here.

</Steps>

<ActivityQuestion>
Time-boxing is one of the few mechanisms in incident response that explicitly inserts a System 2 checkpoint into a System 1 flow. Looking at your answer in step 4, why is the boundary moment (the clock striking the time-box) often more valuable than the investigation itself? What does the responder do at that boundary that they could not do at any other point in the response?
</ActivityQuestion>

## Step 6: The Blameless Postmortem (about 20 minutes)

The technical incident ended on February 1. The learning work began afterward. This is the part of incident response that distinguishes teams that improve from teams that simply survive. Your final task is to produce the skeleton of the postmortem the GitLab team would have written, on paper, with your name on it.

### 6a: Five Whys (about 10 minutes)

The 5 Whys technique is simple to describe and hard to do well. The discipline is to keep asking "why?" until you reach a cause that is **structural** (something about the system, the tools, the process, or the culture), not merely proximate ("the engineer made a mistake"). A 5 Whys that stops at human error has not yet found anything actionable.

<Steps>

1. On the back of your paper, label section 6a **5 Whys**. Draw two columns: **Chain A: The primary was wiped** and **Chain B: Recovery took 18 hours**.

2. In Chain A, start with the symptom and ask "why?" five times. The first level is given: **the primary was wiped because the engineer ran `rm -rf` on the wrong server**. Continue from there. Resist the urge to end at "the engineer was tired" or "they made a mistake"; push past those answers to what the system, the tooling, or the operational practice allowed.

3. In Chain B, do the same starting from **recovery took 18 hours because the only usable backup was 6 hours old and lived on slow storage**. Again, push past "backups were broken" to what allowed the broken state to persist undetected for so long.

4. Circle the **final answer** in each chain. The circled answers should be statements about the system, not about an individual. If your final answer names a person, push one level further.

</Steps>

### 6b: Action Items (about 10 minutes)

A postmortem with elegant analysis and no action items has produced nothing. Each action item should be specific enough to be a ticket, small enough to plausibly complete, and pointed at the structural causes you just identified.

<Steps>

1. Below your 5 Whys, label section 6b **Action Items**. Number 1 through 5. For each item, leave room to write one owner and one due date.

2. Write three action items pointed at your Chain A structural cause. For each, label it as a **system change** (a change to code, tooling, or infrastructure), a **process change** (a change to runbooks, checklists, or workflow), or a **cultural change** (a change to norms, expectations, or training). Then assign exactly one owner role or team and give it a due date.

3. Write two more action items pointed at your Chain B structural cause, again labeled, each with exactly one owner and a due date.

4. Look at the distribution of system / process / cultural labels in your five action items. Write one sentence answering: **if this distribution describes how your team would actually invest in preventing the next incident, where is the weight, and is that the right place for it?**

5. At the bottom right corner of your paper, write one sentence naming who will check these five items in three weeks. Then sign your name and write today's date. This is now your postmortem skeleton: a written artifact, on your own page, that you can return to and refine. The signature marks it as real work; the three-week follow-up is what keeps the action items from becoming theatrical.

</Steps>

<ActivityQuestion>
The lecture argues that blameless culture is not a generous gesture but a structural precondition for getting honest information out of responders. Look at your Chain A 5 Whys. If the engineer who ran the wrong command knew they would be blamed publicly, which of your "why" answers would they have been least likely to volunteer? What does that tell you about why blame and learning are operationally incompatible?
</ActivityQuestion>

## Going Further

You have walked the GitLab incident through every framework the lecture introduced, on a single page, in 80 minutes. The natural next step is to read the source material: GitLab's <a href="https://about.gitlab.com/blog/postmortem-of-database-outage-of-january-31/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">official postmortem</a> is one of the most thorough and honest public incident write-ups in the industry, and reading it after doing this analysis will let you compare your framework decisions to the ones the actual team made. Plan about thirty minutes for a careful read.

After that, the cleanest exercise to build on this one is to pick a different public postmortem and run the same six-step framework against it on a fresh sheet of paper. The <a href="https://github.com/danluu/post-mortems" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">danluu/post-mortems</a> repository is the canonical index; the Cloudflare 2019 regex outage, the AWS S3 2017 typo outage, and the Knight Capital 2012 trading loss are all rich enough to support the same analysis and short enough to read in one sitting. The framework is the same; the failure modes are different, and the contrast is where the lecture's concepts settle into operational instinct.

If you want to make this a live drill rather than a tabletop, the <a href="https://response.pagerduty.com/training/courses/incident_commander/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">PagerDuty Incident Commander training course</a> is the industry-standard short program for the IC role specifically. Working through it after this activity will turn the IC posture you adopted on paper into something you can sustain through a real page.
