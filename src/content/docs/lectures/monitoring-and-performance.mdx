The moment you deploy a service to production, it starts changing. Memory usage climbs or falls. Requests succeed or fail. Pods restart. Disk fills. Most of the time, none of this crosses a threshold that causes user-visible problems. When it does, the question that matters is whether you find out from your tools or from a user filing a complaint.

Operational visibility is the discipline of instrumenting your systems so that you can answer "is my service healthy right now?" without logging into a server and looking around. This lecture covers how to build that visibility: what to measure, how to collect and visualize it, how to define what "healthy" means in measurable terms, and how to design alerts that notify you when things go wrong without training engineers to ignore them. Along the way, it introduces Prometheus and Grafana, the open-source tools that anchor modern Kubernetes monitoring stacks, and explains how those tools work inside a cluster rather than just as standalone processes.

## The Three Pillars of Observability

The observability community has converged on three categories of telemetry data, often called the three pillars: metrics, logs, and traces. These are not interchangeable; each answers a different kind of question about your system, and each is collected and stored differently.

**Metrics** are numeric measurements collected at regular intervals: CPU utilization every 15 seconds, request count per minute, memory usage every 30 seconds. Because they are numeric and sampled uniformly, metrics are cheap to store and fast to query. They are excellent for answering "how much" and "how often" over time, which makes them the backbone of dashboards and alerting. A spike in error rate, a gradual climb in memory utilization, a sudden drop in request throughput: all of these reveal themselves clearly in a time-series graph of metrics.

**Logs** are timestamped records of discrete events. When a request fails with a 500 error, a log entry captures the error message, the request path, the client IP, and often a stack trace. When a database connection is refused, the application writes a log entry with the specific host and port it could not reach. Logs are event-driven rather than interval-driven: they are written when something happens, not on a fixed schedule. That event-driven nature is what makes logs irreplaceable for diagnosis. Metrics tell you that something is wrong; logs tell you why. The Log Management and Incident Investigation lecture covers the tools and techniques for working with logs in depth.

**Traces** follow a single request as it passes through multiple services. A trace might show that a particular API call spent 5 ms in the load balancer, 80 ms in the application server, and 340 ms waiting on a database query. In a single-service application on one server, you do not need distributed tracing because all the timing information is visible in one place. As architecture grows to include multiple services that call each other, tracing becomes essential for identifying which service in the chain is the bottleneck. Tools like [Jaeger](https://www.jaegertracing.io/), [Tempo](https://grafana.com/oss/tempo/), and the OpenTelemetry ecosystem collect and visualize traces. This course does not cover tracing tooling in depth, but knowing the pillar exists helps you understand where it fits relative to metrics and logs.

Most production monitoring systems use all three pillars together. Metrics detect the problem and alert the on-call engineer. Logs provide the context for diagnosing the root cause. Traces (in distributed systems) reveal which service in the chain is responsible. This lecture focuses on the metrics pillar and the alerting and visualization layer that sits on top of it.

## Monitoring vs. Observability

The terms "monitoring" and "observability" are sometimes used interchangeably, but they describe different capabilities. Understanding the difference matters for how you think about instrumenting your systems.

**Monitoring** answers predefined questions. You decide in advance what to measure (CPU usage, request latency, error count), set thresholds, and receive alerts when those thresholds are crossed. Monitoring is essential, but it can only tell you about failure modes you anticipated. If your monitoring configuration watches for high memory usage but not for a slowly growing connection pool leak, you will not know about the leak until it causes the very memory spike you were watching for.

**Observability** is the ability to ask new questions of your system without deploying new instrumentation. A well-instrumented, observable system emits enough data that you can diagnose unexpected failure modes from the outside. You can investigate a failure mode you never anticipated, because the data to answer questions about it already exists.

In practice, this distinction shapes what data you collect. A system that only exposes aggregate error counts is monitorable: you can alert when the error rate climbs. A system that also exposes structured logs with per-request fields, per-endpoint latency histograms, and per-tenant resource consumption is observable: you can ask arbitrary diagnostic questions after the fact. Building toward observability means prioritizing rich, structured data over minimal telemetry, even when you do not yet have a specific question in mind for all of it.

Neither monitoring nor observability is a binary state. Every production system lives somewhere on a spectrum between fully blind and fully observable. The tools and practices in this lecture help you move toward the observable end of that spectrum.

## What to Measure: USE and RED

With hundreds of possible metrics available on a typical Linux server, deciding what to measure can feel overwhelming. Two frameworks cut through this problem. Together, they cover the entire surface of a production system without requiring you to collect everything.

### The USE Method

The USE method, developed by performance engineer Brendan Gregg, applies to physical and logical resources: CPU, memory, disk, network interfaces, file descriptors, and any other constrained resource your system depends on. For each resource, it asks three questions.

**Utilization** is the fraction of the resource's capacity currently in use. For CPU, this is the percentage of time the processor is not idle. For memory, it is the percentage of RAM occupied. For a connection pool, it is the fraction of connections in use. Utilization tells you how close to capacity you are.

**Saturation** is whether work is queuing because the resource is overloaded. A CPU running at 95% utilization with a run queue of 1 process is busy but not saturated. The same CPU with a run queue of 30 processes is saturated: work is waiting. A network interface at 95% utilization with no packet drops is fine; one with rising packet drops is saturated. Saturation is often the more actionable signal, because it indicates that users or processes are waiting, not just that the resource is busy.

**Errors** are the rate at which the resource produces failure signals. Disk read errors, ECC memory corrections (which signal hardware degradation), network packet drops at the interface level, and file descriptor exhaustion all fall here. Errors often signal hardware or configuration problems that utilization alone would never reveal.

The USE method is systematic: you apply it to every resource in your system and collect metrics for all three questions. Doing this before an incident gives you the baseline data to recognize anomalies. In a Kubernetes environment, the resources include not just the node hardware but also the Kubernetes scheduler (are pods queuing because nodes have no capacity?) and the container runtime (are containers hitting their memory or CPU limits?).

### The RED Method

The RED method, developed by Tom Wilkie, applies to request-driven services: web servers, APIs, microservices, databases, and any component that handles requests. For each service, it measures three things.

**Rate** is the number of requests per second the service handles. Rate gives you a baseline: if you know the service normally handles 200 requests per second on a Tuesday afternoon, a drop to 20 requests per second is a signal that traffic is not reaching the service, even if the service itself appears healthy. Rate changes often precede other signals.

**Errors** is the proportion of requests that fail. This is your user-facing availability signal. A healthy service has an error rate near zero; a degraded service has a rate that climbs. Errors should be measured at the layer closest to the user (the load balancer or API gateway) to capture failures from any component in the stack.

**Duration** is how long requests take, measured as a distribution rather than a single average. The average request duration hides the shape of the distribution entirely. A service with a median latency of 80 ms but a 99th-percentile latency of 4 seconds has a serious tail-latency problem. The vast majority of requests are fast, but a predictable fraction of users experience a degraded experience. Percentiles (p50, p95, p99) reveal this; averages conceal it.

USE and RED together cover the entire surface of a typical system. USE tells you whether resources are healthy; RED tells you whether services are behaving correctly from the perspective of the callers that depend on them.

## SLIs, SLOs, and Error Budgets

Knowing what to measure is only half the problem. You also need to define what "healthy enough" looks like. That is where Service Level Indicators, Service Level Objectives, and error budgets come in.

A **Service Level Indicator (SLI)** is a specific, measurable signal that reflects user experience. SLIs are always expressed as ratios: good events divided by total events. For a web application, typical SLIs include availability (the proportion of requests that return a successful response) and latency (the proportion of requests served faster than a defined threshold). The ratio framing matters: it scales correctly across traffic levels. An absolute count of errors does not; 100 errors per minute might be catastrophic at 200 requests per minute but negligible at 200,000 requests per minute.

A **Service Level Objective (SLO)** is a target for an SLI over a rolling time window. An SLO might state that 99.9% of requests will return a non-error response over any 30-day rolling window. That sounds generous until you calculate what 0.1% downtime means: approximately 43 minutes of total error exposure per month. A 99.99% SLO gives you only 4.3 minutes. The math makes SLOs concrete in a way that informal goals like "the service should be reliable" do not.

A **Service Level Agreement (SLA)** is a formal commitment to an SLO, often with contractual and financial consequences. SLAs are what vendors promise to enterprise customers. SLOs are what engineering teams set internally. The internal SLO is typically set more conservatively than the SLA, so that a near-miss on the internal target does not immediately constitute a contractual breach.

The **error budget** is the complement of the SLO. If your SLO is 99.9%, your error budget is 0.1% of requests over the window. This is not a number to minimize at all costs. It is a budget to spend deliberately. When the budget is healthy, engineering teams can deploy frequently and accept some risk in pursuit of feature velocity. When the budget is nearly exhausted, teams should shift priority to reliability work and slow their deployment pace. This creates a data-driven conversation between product teams (who want speed) and operations teams (who want stability), grounded in actual user impact rather than intuition or organizational politics.

<Aside type="note" title="SLOs Require Organizational Commitment">
An error budget only creates the right incentives if the organization actually enforces it. An SLO that nobody acts on when breached is just a number on a dashboard. The most important step after defining an SLO is agreeing on what happens when the error budget burns down faster than expected.
</Aside>

## Prometheus: Architecture and the Pull Model

Prometheus is the most widely deployed open-source metrics system, and it is the standard foundation for monitoring in Kubernetes environments. Understanding its architecture helps you reason about what it can and cannot do, and why it behaves the way it does.

Prometheus operates on a **pull model**: instead of applications pushing metrics to a central collector, Prometheus periodically fetches (scrapes) metrics from HTTP endpoints exposed by your services. Each endpoint returns metrics in the Prometheus exposition format: a plain-text response where each line describes a metric name, a set of labels, and a current value. A typical endpoint response looks like this:

```
# HELP node_memory_MemAvailable_bytes Memory information field MemAvailable_bytes.
# TYPE node_memory_MemAvailable_bytes gauge
node_memory_MemAvailable_bytes 3.52122624e+09
# HELP http_requests_total Total HTTP requests received.
# TYPE http_requests_total counter
http_requests_total{status="200",method="GET"} 14523
http_requests_total{status="500",method="GET"} 42
```

The pull model has a property that push models do not: the absence of data is itself a signal. If Prometheus scrapes a target and receives no response, it knows the target is unreachable. A push-based system, by contrast, cannot distinguish a target that is quietly healthy and not pushing from a target that has crashed and stopped sending data.

<HistoricalNote title="From MRTG to Prometheus: Thirty Years of Metrics (1994–2015)">
The first widely-used network monitoring tool was MRTG (Multi Router Traffic Grapher), written in 1994 by Tobias Oetiker to visualize bandwidth on university routers. It produced static PNG graphs updated every five minutes. Nagios (1999) added alerting and service health checks. Graphite (2006, developed at Etsy) introduced a time-series database purpose-built for metrics; StatsD (2011, also Etsy) made it trivial for application code to emit numeric measurements. Prometheus was created at SoundCloud in 2012 by engineers who missed Borgmon, Google's internal monitoring system, and wanted something similar for their infrastructure. They open-sourced it in 2015, and it became the default metrics layer for Kubernetes, eventually joining the Cloud Native Computing Foundation. Grafana was written by Torkel Ödegaard in 2013 because he wanted better dashboards for Graphite than its built-in interface could provide. Both projects are now core infrastructure for the majority of Kubernetes-based production environments worldwide.
</HistoricalNote>

### Metric Types

Prometheus defines four metric types, each suited to a different measurement pattern.

A **counter** is a value that only increases (or resets to zero when the process restarts). Total HTTP requests served, total bytes sent, and total errors are all counters. You almost never look at the raw counter value; instead, you use the `rate()` function to convert the counter into a per-second rate over a time window. The monotonic property of counters lets Prometheus handle process restarts gracefully: a reset to zero is detectable, and `rate()` automatically accounts for it.

A **gauge** is a value that can go up or down at any time. Current memory usage, number of active database connections, and queue depth are all gauges. Gauges represent a snapshot of state at a moment in time.

A **histogram** samples observations (such as request durations or response sizes) and counts how many fall into each configured bucket. For example, you might configure buckets at 10 ms, 50 ms, 100 ms, 500 ms, and 1 s. A histogram lets you compute quantiles at query time using the `histogram_quantile()` function. The tradeoff is that bucket boundaries must be configured in advance, so you need a reasonable prior belief about the distribution's range.

A **summary** is similar to a histogram but computes quantiles on the client side. Summaries cannot be aggregated across instances (the p99 latency from one server cannot be combined with the p99 from another to give a correct fleet-wide p99), which makes them less useful in distributed deployments. Histograms are generally preferred in modern Prometheus usage.

### Exporters

Most software does not expose Prometheus metrics natively. **Exporters** bridge this gap: separate processes that translate a target system's native instrumentation into Prometheus exposition format. [Node Exporter](https://prometheus.io/docs/guides/node-exporter/) exposes Linux operating system metrics from the host kernel: CPU usage, memory, disk I/O, network throughput, and filesystem usage. The PostgreSQL exporter translates database statistics into Prometheus metrics. Dozens of community-maintained exporters cover databases, message brokers, web servers, cloud services, and hardware.

Exporters run alongside your application, typically as a sidecar container in Kubernetes or a separate process on a VM. When you add a new service to your infrastructure, your first question should be whether a Prometheus exporter already exists for it.

## Prometheus Inside Kubernetes

Running Prometheus as a standalone process works well for a handful of static servers, but it requires manual configuration: you edit `prometheus.yml` with the IP addresses of your scrape targets and reload the process when anything changes. In a Kubernetes environment, services come and go, pods get rescheduled to different IP addresses, and scaling events can add or remove instances in seconds. Maintaining a static scrape configuration under these conditions is impractical.

The Kubernetes ecosystem addressed this with the **Prometheus Operator**, a Kubernetes controller that manages Prometheus as a native Kubernetes resource. Rather than editing a configuration file, you define scrape targets and alert rules as Kubernetes Custom Resources. The Operator watches for these resources and reconciles Prometheus configuration automatically, without requiring a reload or a restart.

**ServiceMonitor** is a Custom Resource Definition (CRD) that tells the Prometheus Operator which Kubernetes Services to scrape, on which port, and at what interval. When you create a ServiceMonitor object with `kubectl apply`, the Operator detects it and adds the corresponding scrape configuration to Prometheus automatically. The Service being monitored does not need to know anything about Prometheus; the ServiceMonitor is a separate object that describes the relationship.

**PrometheusRule** is a CRD that defines alert rules. Instead of YAML files on disk loaded by the Prometheus process, alert definitions live as Kubernetes objects. You create them with `kubectl apply`, view them with `kubectl get prometheusrule`, and manage their lifecycle with the same tools you use for any other manifest. This treats monitoring configuration as infrastructure code, subject to version control, code review, and automated deployment just like application manifests.

The **kube-prometheus-stack** is a [Helm](https://helm.sh/) chart that bundles Prometheus, the Prometheus Operator, Alertmanager, Grafana, kube-state-metrics, and Node Exporter into a single installable package, pre-configured to monitor a Kubernetes cluster immediately after installation. When you install the chart, you get working dashboards for node health, pod health, and cluster resource consumption within minutes, without writing a single ServiceMonitor or PrometheusRule by hand.

### kube-state-metrics

Node Exporter exposes metrics from the Linux kernel: CPU seconds, memory bytes, disk I/O counters, network packets. These are operating system metrics, not Kubernetes-level metrics. Node Exporter does not know whether the process consuming memory is a Kubernetes pod or a system daemon; it just reports what the kernel sees.

**kube-state-metrics** fills the gap by querying the Kubernetes API server and exposing cluster state as Prometheus metrics. It answers questions like: how many replicas does this Deployment have right now versus how many are desired? Has this pod been restarted in the last five minutes? Is this PersistentVolumeClaim bound? These are Kubernetes-level facts, not visible to the operating system.

kube-state-metrics reads probe outcomes from the API server and exposes them as metrics. A pod that is failing its readiness probe shows up as `kube_pod_status_ready{condition="true"} == 0`, and a pod that the kubelet has restarted multiple times shows up as an increasing `kube_pod_container_status_restarts_total`. These are the metrics behind the most actionable Kubernetes alert rules.

When you write a PromQL query that uses `kube_pod_container_status_restarts_total`, you are reading data from kube-state-metrics. When you use `node_memory_MemAvailable_bytes`, you are reading from Node Exporter. Knowing the origin of each metric helps you reason about what is and is not being measured when something appears missing from your data.

## PromQL: Extracting Meaning from Metrics

PromQL is the query language for extracting meaning from raw Prometheus data. A few functions cover the majority of practical use cases. Understanding them deeply is more valuable than knowing many functions shallowly.

### Instant and Range Vectors

A plain metric name in PromQL returns an **instant vector**: the most recent value for every time series matching that name, along with its labels. Labels are key-value pairs that differentiate time series. A single metric like `http_requests_total` might match dozens of time series, each with a different combination of label values such as `{status="200", method="GET", job="api"}` and `{status="500", method="POST", job="api"}`.

Adding a time window in square brackets produces a **range vector**: the historical values for each matching time series over the specified duration. A range vector is the input to functions like `rate()` and `increase()`, which need a window of history to compute rates of change.

### rate() and increase()

`rate()` computes the per-second average rate of increase of a counter over a time window. It is the correct function to apply to counters, because the raw counter value grows indefinitely and is not directly interpretable.

```promql
rate(http_requests_total[5m])
```

This returns the average per-second request rate over the last 5 minutes, separately for each label combination. The 5-minute window smooths out short bursts; a shorter window like `[1m]` is more responsive but noisier.

`increase()` returns the total increase in a counter over a time window, rather than a per-second rate. It answers "how many times did this event occur in the last 10 minutes?" A PromQL expression for detecting crash-looping pods:

```promql
increase(kube_pod_container_status_restarts_total{namespace="default"}[10m]) > 3
```

A value above 3 means a container restarted more than 3 times in the last 10 minutes. This is a natural basis for a crash-loop alert.

### histogram_quantile()

For histograms, `histogram_quantile()` computes percentiles at query time. The first argument is the quantile (0.95 for the 95th percentile, 0.99 for the 99th) and the second is a range expression over the bucket metric:

```promql
histogram_quantile(0.95, rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket[5m]))
```

This is how you build a latency SLI from Prometheus. The 95th-percentile latency is the request duration that 95% of requests complete within. Plotting p50, p95, and p99 on the same panel reveals the shape of the latency distribution and makes tail-latency problems immediately visible.

### Aggregation

PromQL aggregation operators summarize across label dimensions. `sum()` adds values; `avg()` computes their average; `topk()` returns the highest N values. The `by` clause controls which labels survive the aggregation:

```promql
sum(rate(http_requests_total[5m])) by (job)
```

This computes total request rate across all instances, grouped by the `job` label (the service name). It is the standard query for a top-level traffic panel on an overview dashboard.

To compute memory usage as a percentage of total RAM on the node:

```promql
100 - (node_memory_MemAvailable_bytes / node_memory_MemTotal_bytes * 100)
```

This subtracts available memory from 100% to get the percentage in use. The formula works regardless of how much total RAM the node has.

## Grafana: From Numbers to Understanding

Prometheus stores and queries data; Grafana makes it visual. A Grafana dashboard is a collection of panels, each displaying one or more PromQL queries as a graph, stat, gauge, table, or heatmap. The power of a dashboard is not in any individual panel but in the layout of many panels together, so that relationships between signals are immediately visible without switching between multiple pages.

### The Golden Signals

Google's Site Reliability Engineering book distilled monitoring into four signals that apply to any request-driven service, calling them the golden signals. These overlap substantially with the RED method but add a fourth signal that RED omits.

**Latency** measures how long successful requests take, distinguished from how long failed requests take. A failure that completes in 1 ms should not be averaged with slow successful requests; the two have different operational meanings. Latency should be measured at percentiles (p50, p95, p99), not as an average.

**Traffic** measures demand on the system: requests per second, queries per second, or whatever unit best represents the system's load. Traffic context is essential for interpreting other signals. High CPU at 100 requests per second may indicate a problem; high CPU at 10,000 requests per second may be entirely expected.

**Errors** measure the rate of requests that fail, both explicitly (HTTP 500, connection refused, database error) and implicitly (HTTP 200 with incorrect or empty content, when that can be detected). Error rate is the user-facing availability signal and the most direct measure of whether the service is working.

**Saturation** measures how full the service is: what proportion of its capacity it is consuming. For a CPU, it is utilization. For a connection pool, it is the fraction of connections in use. For a message queue, it is the queue depth relative to its configured maximum. Saturation is often the leading indicator: it starts climbing before latency and errors do.

When all four golden signals are healthy, you can have reasonable confidence that the service is working well for users. When one is degraded, the others provide context for understanding why.

### Dashboard Design Principles

A dashboard that tries to show everything ends up communicating nothing. These principles separate dashboards that engineers actually use from ones that exist but are never opened.

Put the user perspective first. The top row of any service dashboard should answer the question a user would ask: is the service available and fast? Lead with error rate and latency percentiles before showing infrastructure metrics. If those panels are green, everything below is context. If they are red, the panels below help you locate the source.

Layer your dashboards. A single dashboard covering all services at all levels of detail creates visual noise. An overview dashboard shows one row per major service, each with its golden signals. Clicking a service takes you to a detailed dashboard for that component. On-call engineers start at the overview and drill down to the component that is failing.

Keep every panel purposeful. Each panel on a dashboard should answer a specific operational question. A panel that exists "just in case" someone wants to look at it occupies space that could be used for something actionable. After each incident, add the panels that would have helped you diagnose it faster, and remove the ones nobody looked at.

Add annotations for deployments and incidents. Grafana supports vertical markers on time-series graphs that show when a deployment happened or a configuration change was applied. A spike in errors immediately following a deployment annotation is one of the clearest causal signals available in a dashboard, and it is visible in seconds.

Use consistent units and scales across related panels. Two latency panels in the same row should use the same y-axis unit and scale. Mismatched scales make it impossible to visually correlate events across panels, which is the main reason dashboards exist.

## Alerting: From Metrics to Action

Dashboards are for engineers who happen to be looking at them. Alerts are for the rest of the time. The purpose of an alerting system is to notify the right person about the right problem at the right time, with enough context to take action.

### Alert Rules

A Prometheus alert rule has three required parts: a PromQL expression that defines the triggering condition, a `for` duration that specifies how long the condition must be true before the alert fires, and labels and annotations that describe the alert and carry information to the responder.

The `for` clause is frequently misunderstood. Without it, a single bad scrape, a momentary CPU spike, or a brief network blip would fire an alert immediately. The `for` clause prevents this by requiring the condition to be continuously true for the specified duration before the alert transitions from Pending to Firing. A `for: 2m` clause means the condition must hold at every scrape interval for at least two minutes. This dramatically reduces false positives while still catching sustained problems promptly.

```yaml
groups:
  - name: example.rules
    rules:
      - alert: PodCrashLooping
        expr: increase(kube_pod_container_status_restarts_total{namespace="default"}[10m]) > 3
        for: 2m
        labels:
          severity: critical
        annotations:
          summary: "Pod {{ $labels.pod }} is crash looping"
          description: "Pod {{ $labels.pod }} has restarted more than 3 times in the last 10 minutes."
```

The `{{ $labels.pod }}` template syntax allows alert annotations to include the specific label values that triggered the alert, so the notified engineer knows which pod, which node, or which service is involved without having to look it up.

### Symptom-Based vs. Cause-Based Alerts

The most important design principle for production alerts is the distinction between symptom-based and cause-based alerts.

A **cause-based alert** fires on an internal metric: "CPU utilization above 80%," "disk I/O wait above 15%," "connection pool 90% full." These alerts are fragile because they require anticipating every internal cause of a user-visible problem in advance. They also fire even when the cause has no user impact: a disk at 85% capacity is not always urgent. And they frequently create false positives during expected high-traffic periods, training engineers to associate high CPU with false alarms.

A **symptom-based alert** fires on a user-facing signal: "error rate above 1% for 5 minutes," "p95 latency above 500 ms for 3 minutes," "service probe failing." These alerts fire only when users are actually experiencing a problem, regardless of which internal cause is responsible. They have fewer false positives and are always actionable, because they always indicate real impact.

The practical approach is a small number of symptom-based alerts for page-worthy problems (things that wake someone up at 2 AM) supplemented by lower-urgency cause-based alerts for conditions that could become problems but have not yet caused user impact. High disk usage trending upward is worth a warning; it is not worth waking anyone up until it becomes critical.

### Runbooks

Every alert should be paired with a runbook: a short document that tells the on-call engineer what to check first and what to do. A runbook does not need to cover every possible root cause. It covers the most likely ones and the first-response steps that apply regardless of cause.

A runbook for a pod crash-looping alert might say: run `kubectl describe pod <pod-name>` and examine the Events section at the bottom. If the image pull failed, check that the image tag exists in the registry. If the container exits immediately after starting, run `kubectl logs <pod-name> --previous` to read the last known error from the terminated container. If the exit code is 137, the container was killed by the OOM killer and needs higher memory limits. If a ConfigMap or Secret changed recently, check whether the configuration change introduced an invalid value.

These steps narrow the search space dramatically and allow an engineer unfamiliar with the service to take productive action within the first minutes of an incident. A runbook that must be written from scratch while on call is not a runbook; it is a delay.

### Alert Fatigue and How to Prevent It

Alert fatigue is the condition in which engineers receive so many alert notifications that they begin treating all of them as background noise, including the ones that are real. It is a systemic reliability risk: a team experiencing alert fatigue is effectively operating without monitoring, because the monitoring infrastructure is present but the human attention it depends on has been eroded.

Alert fatigue has one cause: alerts that do not consistently warrant the response they demand. An alert that fires every night at 2:47 AM and always resolves without action by 3:00 AM is training engineers to silence it. An alert that fires whenever a batch job runs and briefly spikes CPU is training engineers to ignore CPU alerts. Every alert that fires without prompting a response represents a withdrawal from the account of trust that the entire alerting system depends on.

Preventing alert fatigue requires treating noisy alerts as bugs. Track alert volume over time. Any alert that fires more than twice per shift without producing an actionable response should be redesigned or removed. Any alert that is routinely silenced during maintenance windows should have silence rules built into its configuration. The goal is not to have fewer alerts; it is to have alerts that are always meaningful.

<Aside type="caution" title="Alert Fatigue Is a Safety Problem">
When engineers are trained to ignore alerts, the false negative rate of the entire alerting system climbs toward 100%. The next real incident is the one they ignore because it looks like all the noisy ones. Treating alert fatigue as a reliability issue, not a comfort issue, is the correct framing.
</Aside>

### Severity, Routing, and On-Call Culture

**Alertmanager** is the component that receives fired alerts from Prometheus and routes them to notification channels based on labels. A common severity scheme uses three levels.

A **critical** alert means user-facing impact is occurring or imminent. The on-call engineer receives a page immediately, regardless of time. An availability SLO breach, a database that is down, or a pod that cannot start all fall here. Critical alerts should be rare enough that every one is investigated seriously.

A **warning** alert means something is degrading and needs attention within business hours, but users are not yet experiencing a problem. Memory trending upward, a certificate expiring in 14 days, or disk usage above 75% are warnings. They go to a Slack channel or create a ticket.

An **info** notification is notable but not actionable: a deployment completing, a scheduled job finishing, or a scale event occurring. Info notifications are logged but do not interrupt anyone.

The goal of the severity hierarchy is to ensure that the on-call rotation is sustainable. On-call engineers who are regularly paged multiple times per shift burn out quickly and start making mistakes during incidents. A target of no more than two pages per shift, each requiring real response, is a common operational standard. Tracking this metric and treating it as a reliability indicator is part of maintaining a healthy on-call rotation.

### SLO Burn-Rate Alerting

The traditional approach to SLO alerting is to alert when the SLO is violated: if your availability SLO is 99.9%, alert when error rate exceeds 0.1%. The problem is that this approach is reactive by design. You only get notified after you have already breached the objective.

A more sophisticated and increasingly industry-standard approach is **burn-rate alerting**: alerting when your error budget is being consumed faster than it can be replenished, before you have exhausted it. The error budget for a 99.9% SLO over 30 days is 43.8 minutes. If errors are occurring at a rate that would exhaust the entire budget in one hour, something is very wrong even if the SLO has not technically been violated yet.

The burn rate is the ratio of current error rate to the SLO's acceptable error rate. A burn rate of 1 means you are consuming the budget at exactly the sustainable pace: at 99.9% availability, one error per thousand requests, budget is gone in 30 days. A burn rate of 14 means you are consuming the budget 14 times faster than sustainable: the entire 30-day budget would be gone in about 2 days.

```promql
(
  rate(http_requests_total{status=~"5.."}[1h])
  /
  rate(http_requests_total[1h])
) / (1 - 0.999)
```

This query computes the current burn rate against a 99.9% SLO. A value above 14 is a common threshold for a critical page; a value above 1 for a sustained period is worth a warning. Using two different windows (a short window like 5 minutes for fast response, a longer window like 1 hour for sustained burn) and requiring both to be elevated reduces false positives from transient spikes. The Google SRE Workbook covers this pattern in detail.

Burn-rate alerting represents a shift in how you think about SLOs: not as limits to enforce after the fact, but as budgets to manage in real time.

### Alertmanager Routing in Practice

Alertmanager receives alerts from Prometheus and routes them to the right destinations based on label matchers. A complete routing configuration might look like this:

```yaml
route:
  group_by: ['alertname', 'namespace']
  group_wait: 30s
  group_interval: 5m
  repeat_interval: 4h
  receiver: 'default-slack'
  routes:
    - match:
        severity: critical
      receiver: 'pagerduty'
    - match:
        severity: warning
      receiver: 'warning-slack'

receivers:
  - name: 'pagerduty'
    pagerduty_configs:
      - routing_key: '<your-integration-key>'
  - name: 'warning-slack'
    slack_configs:
      - api_url: 'https://hooks.slack.com/services/...'
        channel: '#alerts-warning'
  - name: 'default-slack'
    slack_configs:
      - api_url: 'https://hooks.slack.com/services/...'
        channel: '#alerts-info'
```

The `group_by` field clusters related alerts into a single notification rather than sending one notification per firing alert. Without grouping, a cascading failure that triggers 20 alerts simultaneously would send 20 separate pages. With `group_by: ['alertname', 'namespace']`, those 20 alerts are batched into a few grouped notifications based on their common properties.

`group_wait` is how long Alertmanager waits before sending the initial notification for a new group of alerts, allowing time for additional related alerts to arrive and be included. `group_interval` is how long it waits before sending a follow-up notification if the group state changes. `repeat_interval` is how often a still-firing alert is re-notified.

Silences are a planned feature, not a workaround: you create a silence before a maintenance window so that alerts expected during the maintenance do not page the on-call team. A silence says "during this time window, don't notify for alerts matching these labels," which is different from "ignore these alerts forever."

## Local Tools for Real-Time Investigation

Prometheus and Grafana describe the historical picture. Sometimes you need to understand what is happening on a specific machine at this moment, without waiting for metrics to be scraped and displayed. Linux provides a set of command-line tools for this purpose that require no prior setup and work on any server you can SSH into.

`htop` is an interactive process viewer that shows per-process CPU and memory usage in real time, sortable by any column, with color-coded utilization bars for quick scanning. When a machine is behaving unexpectedly, sorting by CPU (press `P`) or by resident memory (press `M`) frequently identifies the consuming process in seconds. The `RES` column shows how much physical RAM a process is using, as distinct from virtual memory.

`vmstat` reports virtual memory, CPU, and I/O statistics at a specified interval. Running `vmstat 1` prints a new line every second. The columns to watch: `si` and `so` (swap in and swap out) should be zero on a healthy system; any nonzero value indicates the machine is paging to disk under memory pressure. The `wa` column (I/O wait) indicates that CPUs are idle waiting for disk operations to complete. The `us` and `sy` columns show user-space and kernel-space CPU time respectively.

`iostat` provides per-device I/O statistics. With the `-x` flag it adds extended columns: `%util` shows how busy the device is (100% means fully saturated), `await` shows average I/O completion time in milliseconds, and `r/s` and `w/s` show read and write operations per second. A device with 100% utilization and rising `await` is saturated: I/O requests are queuing because the device cannot keep up. This maps directly to the "saturation" question in the USE method.

`ss` (socket statistics) shows network connection state, replacing the older `netstat` command on modern Linux. Running `ss -tnp` shows all TCP connections with associated process names and PIDs. This lets you verify that services are connected to their expected peers, check for connections stuck in unusual states (a large number of `CLOSE_WAIT` connections often indicates a resource leak), and confirm that no unexpected processes have opened network connections.

`strace` attaches to a running process and prints every system call it makes, along with arguments and return values. It is a last-resort diagnostic tool because it adds significant overhead to the traced process, but it is invaluable when nothing else explains the behavior. Running `strace -p <PID> -c` produces a summary of system calls rather than printing every one: you see which calls dominate, how long they take, and how often they fail. A process spending most of its time in `futex()` is waiting on a lock. A process in `read()` or `write()` with long durations is blocked on I/O. A process calling `mmap()` repeatedly may be experiencing memory fragmentation.

The order in which you reach for these tools matters. Start with `htop` and `vmstat` (read-only, negligible overhead). Progress to `iostat` and `ss` if the initial picture is unclear. Reach for `strace` only when you have a specific hypothesis about a specific process and need to confirm it. The more invasive the tool, the more certain you should be that the cost is justified.

These tools are complements to Prometheus, not replacements. Prometheus gives you the historical baseline for detecting anomalies and the long-term trends for understanding gradual degradation. Local tools give you immediate, high-resolution detail for a specific host at a specific moment in time. Effective operators know when to reach for each.

## Takeaways

Operational visibility exists on a spectrum. At one end, a service with no instrumentation fails silently and you find out from users. At the other end, a fully observable system emits rich, structured telemetry that lets you diagnose unexpected failures from the outside without deploying new code. The Prometheus and Grafana stack positions you at a practical point on that spectrum: you get the core infrastructure of modern production monitoring at a manageable operational cost.

USE and RED give you a systematic answer to what to measure. Apply them before you have a production incident to motivate you, not during one. SLIs and SLOs give you a precise vocabulary for what "healthy enough" looks like, and error budgets turn that vocabulary into a decision-making framework for balancing feature velocity against reliability investment.

Prometheus's pull model shapes what you can observe and why. Its Kubernetes integration via ServiceMonitors and PrometheusRules treats monitoring configuration as infrastructure code: versioned, reviewed, and deployed with the same discipline as application manifests. The kube-prometheus-stack bundles this integration into a practical starting point for any cluster.

Alert design is where most monitoring investments fail. A monitoring system full of noisy, cause-based alerts with no runbooks trains engineers to ignore it. A smaller set of carefully designed, symptom-based alerts with clear runbooks and appropriate routing makes the on-call rotation sustainable and keeps response times fast. The cost of a false alert is not just the time spent investigating it; it is the erosion of trust in the entire alerting system.

The Incident Response and Disaster Recovery lecture builds on these foundations, covering what happens after an alert fires: how to coordinate a response, how to communicate during an outage, and how to hand the findings to the postmortem process that makes the next incident less likely.
